  • Apply the evolutionary perspective to explain how a behavior or mental process may have helped humans survive and reproduce, and explain why eugenics is a discredited misuse of ideas about heredity.

Daniela has just started high school, and she has noticed that her heart races before tests and in crowded hallways. Her mother and her grandmother have both been diagnosed with anxiety disorders. Daniela wonders: did she inherit this from them, or did she learn to worry by growing up around people who worry? Psychologists ask questions like hers all the time.


In this lesson, you’ll use the research tools from Unit 0 to explore how genes and experience work together to shape behavior, why evolutionary psychologists think some tendencies are shared by nearly all humans, and how researchers use twin, family, and adoption studies to sort out the influence of each.


Nature and Nurture Work Together


Daniela’s question is a version of the nature–nurture question. Nature refers to heredity, the genes we inherit from our biological parents. Nurture refers to environment: every outside influence on us from before birth onward, such as family, school, culture, and even our physical surroundings. Height, how outgoing we are, and the risk of a psychological disorder all reflect a mix of the two.


AP Exam Tip: You won’t need to explain how genes work on the AP exam, but here’s a quick refresher. Most cells in the human body contain 46 chromosomes, arranged in 23 pairs. Chromosomes are made of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), and genes are segments of DNA that carry instructions for how the body develops. You may remember dominant and recessive genes, but most traits, including eye color and personality, are shaped by many genes working together. What the exam does expect: genes can influence physical, behavioral, and mental traits.


Genetic Predisposition


Daniela’s family history suggests she may have a genetic predisposition for anxiety: a genetic makeup that raises the chance that a person will develop a particular trait or condition. The key word is chance. A predisposition makes a trait more likely than it would be for the average person, but it does not guarantee that the trait will appear.


Whether it appears depends partly on environment. For Daniela, stress, support from family and friends, and the coping skills she learns could all make a difference. Think of a predisposition as a volume knob rather than an on-off switch: genes help set the range, and experiences can turn a tendency up or down.


This is one of the most important ideas in the course: most behavior and mental processes result from nature and nurture interacting, not from either one alone.


Quick Check: Daniela’s cousin Rafa says, “Anxiety runs in our family, so there’s nothing I can do about it.” Use the term genetic predisposition to explain what Rafa is missing.

Rafa is treating a predisposition like a guarantee. A family history of anxiety suggests he may have a genetic predisposition, which makes an anxiety disorder more likely for him than for the average person, but it doesn’t mean he will definitely develop one. His environment, including stress, support from others, and the coping skills he practices, also affects whether that tendency shows up. Nature and nurture work together.

The Evolutionary Perspective


Genes and environment help explain why Daniela differs from her classmates. But nearly everyone feels anxious sometimes. Why would fear and worry be part of human nature at all?


As you learned in Lesson 0.1, the evolutionary perspective explores how natural selection may have shaped behavior and mental processes that helped our ancestors survive and reproduce. It builds on Charles Darwin’s principle of natural selection. Individuals vary, and resources are limited. Those whose traits help them survive and reproduce tend to leave more offspring, so the genes behind those traits are more likely to be passed on, and over many generations the traits become more common. Notice that populations change over generations; individuals don’t evolve during their lifetimes.


Here’s how an evolutionary psychologist might think about Daniela’s racing heart. An ancestor who felt a jolt of alarm at a rustle in the grass may have reacted to a predator faster than one who stayed calm. If that quick fear response helped people survive long enough to have children, it could have become common in the population. So a capacity for anxiety may have had adaptive value, even if today it also shows up before a history test.


Evolutionary psychologists use this reasoning to propose explanations for similarities across humans, such as why people everywhere tend to fear heights. These explanations are hypotheses that can be hard to test, so a careful explanation says a behavior may have been adaptive, not that every behavior must have an evolutionary purpose.


Quick Check: Many people crave sweet and high-fat foods, even though these foods are easy to overeat today. How might an evolutionary psychologist explain this preference?

An evolutionary psychologist would point to natural selection. For most of human history, food was scarce, and sweet or fatty foods provided a lot of energy. Ancestors who were drawn to these foods may have been more likely to survive and reproduce, passing that preference on to their offspring. Over many generations, the preference became common in the human population. Today, food is much easier to get, but we’ve inherited a preference that was adaptive in a very different environment.

A Warning from History: Eugenics


Ideas about heredity and evolution have also been badly misused. Eugenics was a movement that tried to control human reproduction to promote traits its supporters considered desirable and to prevent (or even eliminate) others. It claimed to be scientific, but it rested on biased, inaccurate assumptions about which people were “better,” and it was used to justify discrimination. Eugenics is now discredited, and its history is a warning to watch for anyone who uses psychology to discriminate against others.


How Psychologists Study Nature and Nurture


So how could researchers find out how much of Daniela’s anxiety risk comes from her genes and how much comes from her experiences? They can’t run an experiment, because no one can randomly assign people to genes or families. Instead, they rely on twin studies, family studies, and adoption studies. These are non-experimental (often correlational) designs, like the ones in Lessons 0.2 and 0.4, so they reveal relationships but can’t prove cause and effect on their own.


Twin Studies


Identical twins develop from a single fertilized egg that splits in two, so they share all of their genes. Fraternal twins develop from two separate fertilized eggs, so they share about half of their genes on average, just like any other pair of siblings. If identical twins are more alike on a trait than fraternal twins, genes probably play a role. Twins raised apart are especially useful, since their similarities can’t come from sharing a home.


Example: Psychologist Thomas Bouchard led the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart, which followed more than 100 pairs of twins who were separated early in life and raised in different homes. When the team compared IQ scores, identical twins raised apart had a correlation of about +0.69. Identical twins raised together tend to be even more alike, with correlations around +0.88. Remember from Lesson 0.4 that the closer a correlation is to +1, the stronger the relationship. A correlation of +0.69 between people who never shared a home suggests that genes contribute a lot to IQ scores, and the higher correlation for twins raised together suggests that environment matters, too.


Twin studies have a limitation, though. Identical twins raised apart still look alike, so other people may treat them in similar ways. That similar treatment is part of their environment and could explain some of their similarities, making it harder to separate genes from experience.


Quick Check: Suppose researchers find that when one identical twin has an anxiety disorder, the other twin also has one much more often than in fraternal twin pairs, but far from 100% of the time. What do these findings suggest about nature and nurture?

Because identical twins share all of their genes and fraternal twins share about half, the higher rate for identical twins suggests that genes (nature) contribute to anxiety disorders. But if genes were the whole story, genetically identical twins would always share the diagnosis. Since they don’t, environment (nurture) must matter, too. And because this is a non-experimental study, it shows a relationship, not proof of exactly how genes cause the disorder.

Family and Adoption Studies


Family studies ask whether a trait is more common among close relatives, who share more of their genes, than among distant relatives or the general population. Daniela’s family history is the kind of pattern they look for. But close relatives usually share a home, too, so a family study alone can’t fully separate genes from environment.


Adoption studies help with that problem. They compare adopted children with their biological parents, who share their genes, and with their adoptive parents, who share their environment. If adopted children resemble their biological parents on a trait, that points toward genes; if they resemble their adoptive parents, that points toward environment. This research shows that the home a child grows up in can shape their attitudes, values, manners, politics, education, faith, and risk of some psychological disorders, even though siblings raised together can still turn out quite differently.

Putting It Together


So, did Daniela inherit her anxiety? Psychology’s best answer is “partly”: she may have a genetic predisposition, but her experiences will help determine whether it shows up. In this lesson, you learned that most behavior and mental processes result from nature and nurture interacting, that the evolutionary perspective proposes explanations for tendencies nearly all humans share (and that eugenics misused ideas about heredity), and that twin, family, and adoption studies help researchers estimate the influence of genes and environment.


In Lesson 1.2, you’ll turn to the biological system that makes all of this possible. Whatever role genes and experiences play, their effects on behavior ultimately work through the nervous system.

Original lesson (Google Doc version), for comparison

The role of biology in psychology (also known as the neuroscience, biological, or biopsychological perspective) is becoming more and more important. To really understand modern psychology, you’ll need to know some basic biological principles.

The Interaction of Nature and Nurture

One major biological factor that influences how we think and act is genetics. Traits like height and weight, how introverted or extroverted we are, or the likelihood that we will develop a mental disorder come from a mix of nature (our genes) and nurture (our environment).

Just in case you need a reminder from biology, every human cell has 46 chromosomes organized in 23 pairs. Chromosomes are made of DNA (short for deoxyribonucleic acid), and specific parts of DNA, called genes, control how certain traits develop. Genes can be dominant or recessive. If you inherit two recessive genes for a trait, the recessive trait will show up. But if you have a mix of dominant and recessive genes, the dominant trait will take over. Researchers study how different combinations of genes affect physical traits (like eye color) and behavioral traits (like personality).

For the AP exam, you don’t need to remember the details about how genes work in the human body, but you do need to know that our genes can influence physical, behavioral, and mental traits and processes. If someone’s DNA makes them more likely to develop a certain trait or condition, psychologists say they have a genetic predisposition for that trait or condition. This means they might be more likely than other people to exhibit that trait or condition, but it does not mean that they will definitely exhibit that trait or condition.

Our environment—which includes external factors like family interactions, education, cultural norms, and even our literal physical environment—also plays a role in determining whether or not we will exhibit a trait or condition.

You might think of it like this: if we inherit a genetic predisposition for a trait, behavior, or condition, it’s like we inherited a light switch. Our environment determines whether that switch will be flipped on—meaning we exhibit that trait, behavior, or condition—or off—meaning we do not exhibit that trait, behavior, or condition.

One of the most important things you need to remember as you begin the study of psychology is that most behavioral and mental processes are caused by a combination of nature and nurture.

The Evolutionary Perspective

Closely related to the biological perspective within psychology is the evolutionary perspective. If you recall from Lesson 1 in the previous unit, the evolutionary perspective explores how natural selection affects the expression of behavior and mental processes to increase survival and reproductive success.

This perspective is based on the work of Charles Darwin, particularly his principle of natural selection. The idea is that living things compete for the resources they need to survive, and there are variations in biology and behavior that make organisms (like animals and humans) more likely to survive and reproduce, having offspring and passing their genes on to a new generation. This means that the genes that lead to these successful biological and behavioral traits are more likely to be passed on, and over time, the population can change to be more likely to exhibit these traits.

Evolutionary psychologists use these principles to explain similarities in human behavior like why many women experience nausea in the first three months of pregnancy, why infants start to fear strangers right around the same time they learn to crawl, why people are more likely to have a phobia of snakes or spiders than guns, and why women tend to be pickier than men when choosing sexual partners.

As interesting as the evolutionary perspective can be, it’s important to know that throughout history, some scientists have tried to apply the evolutionary perspective in ways that discriminate against others. This is referred to as eugenics, the now discredited practice of attempting to control human reproduction in ways that promote certain characteristics while preventing (or even eliminating) others. We want to be on guard for practices like this that use psychology to discriminate against others.

How Psychologists Study Nature vs. Nurture

Researchers trying to study the role that genetics and environment play in the development of certain traits or conditions often rely on twin studies, family studies, and adoption studies.

Identical twins, in particular, are super useful for studying the role of genetics. Identical twins come from a single fertilized egg that splits into two, meaning they share 100% of the same DNA (as opposed to fraternal twins, which develop from two separate fertilized eggs and are no more alike than siblings who aren't twins).

Psychologist Thomas Bouchard conducted a famous study where he found over 100 identical twins who were adopted and raised in different families. By comparing these twins, Bouchard and his team studied how genetics and environment influence traits. For example, they looked at IQ scores. Twins raised apart had a correlation coefficient of 0.69 for IQ, while twins raised together had a higher correlation of 0.88. This suggests that the environment does affect IQ since twins raised together had more similar scores. However, the high correlation for twins raised apart shows that genetics plays a big role, too.

As helpful as twin and adoption studies can be, they have faced some criticism. Even when identical twins are raised in different families, they still look very similar. Because of this, people might treat them in similar ways, which could create similar psychological environments for both twins. This shared treatment could explain some of the similarities in traits, making it harder to figure out exactly how much genetics versus environment matters.

Naturally, twin studies are common when studying the effects of heredity and environment because identical twins share the same DNA; however, useful studies have also been carried out by looking at families in general and adoptive families in particular. If you have siblings, you know that not all people from the same family (or raised in the same house) exhibit the same traits. But studies have also shown that the home a child is raised in can impact that child’s attitudes, values, manners, politics, education, faith, and likelihood of developing certain mental disorders.

This brings us back to our key point from this unit: most behavioral and mental processes are caused by a combination of nature and nurture.
