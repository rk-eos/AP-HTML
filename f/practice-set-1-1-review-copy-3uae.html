1. Farida wants to determine the relative effects of nature vs. nurture on an individual’s likelihood of showing symptoms of depression. Which population would be the most helpful in helping her to answer this question?

Measures: Objective 3 · Buzz import number 1

  • A. identical twins ✔ (correct)
    Feedback: Correct! Identical twins share the same DNA, so if Farida were to study twins raised together (same DNA, same environment) and twins raised apart (same DNA, different environment), she would receive helpful information for her study.
  • B. adopted children and their adoptive parents
    Feedback: Adopted children share an environment with their adoptive parents but not their DNA. Without a comparison to people who share their DNA, such as their birth parents, this group alone can’t show the relative effects of nature and nurture. Think about which population would let Farida compare the effects of genes and environment.
  • C. couples who have been married for many years
    Feedback: Couples do not (likely) share any DNA and they have chosen to live together, so there would be too many confounding variables to draw any useful conclusions with this population.
  • D. children who do not have any siblings
    Feedback: Without anyone to compare these children to (either with shared DNA or a shared home environment), children who do not have any siblings would not provide Farida with useful information to answer her question.

2. Tomás conducts personality research, and he hypothesizes that a person’s level of introversion or extroversion is most strongly influenced by heredity. To test his hypothesis, he recruits a wide variety of families and gives them a personality questionnaire to measure their level of introversion or extroversion. Which of the following findings would provide the best evidence to support Tomás’s hypothesis?

Measures: Objective 3 · Buzz import number 2

  • A. Fraternal twins have more similar scores than non-twin siblings.
    Feedback: Fraternal twins have the same likelihood of shared DNA as non-twin siblings, so this finding would not support Tomás’s hypothesis that introversion/extroversion is influenced by heredity.
  • B. Identical twins who were adopted at birth tend to have more similar scores to their birth parents than their adoptive parents. ✔ (correct)
    Feedback: Correct! Identical twins who were adopted at birth have shared DNA but different home environments, so if they are more similar to their birth parents, this would support Tomás’s hypothesis that introversion/extroversion is influenced by heredity.
  • C. Identical twins raised in the same home score less similarly on the personality test than identical twins raised in different homes.
    Feedback: Both groups in this finding are identical twins, so they have the same amount of shared DNA; the only difference between the groups is their environment. A finding that doesn’t compare people with different amounts of shared DNA can’t show that heredity has the strongest influence.
  • D. Fraternal twins raised in the same home score more similarly on the personality tests than fraternal twins raised in different homes.
    Feedback: Both groups in this finding are fraternal twins, so the only difference between them is their environment. If twins raised together are more similar, that points to the influence of environment, not heredity.

3. Mei is conducting a twin study on aggression. The more difference shown in the levels of aggression between identical twins raised apart, the more those differences in aggression can be attributed to their

Measures: Objective 1, Objective 3 · Buzz import number 3

  • A. genetics.
    Feedback: Identical twins share the same DNA, so differences in aggression could not be attributed to genetics.
  • B. evolution.
    Feedback: Evolution happens in a species over many generations, so differences between identical twins could not be attributed to evolution.
  • C. coincidence.
    Feedback: While we would have to run a test of statistical significance to say for sure that the differences in aggression are not due to chance, the fact that the twins share DNA means we can make a tentative conclusion that the differences in aggression may be due to something else.
  • D. environments. ✔ (correct)
    Feedback: Correct! The twins have the same DNA, so any differences in aggression between them cannot be due to genetics, making it likely that their different environments have influenced their levels of aggression.

8. Anika conducted a study and found the correlation coefficient for the IQ scores of identical twins raised separately to be +0.72. The correlation coefficient for non-twin siblings raised separately was roughly +0.24. Anika’s findings suggest that

Measures: Objective 3 · Buzz import number 9

  • A. there is no evidence to support the idea that there is a genetic predisposition for intelligence.
    Feedback: Both groups were raised apart, so their similarity in IQ scores can’t come from sharing a home. Think about what the two groups do share with each other, and how much of it.
  • B. there is a genetic component to intelligence. ✔ (correct)
    Feedback: Correct! Identical twins share all of their DNA, and non-twin siblings share about half. Even though both groups were raised apart, the identical twins’ IQ scores were much more strongly correlated, which suggests that genes contribute to intelligence.
  • C. there is no clear connection between the family a child is raised in and the child’s performance on an intelligence test.
    Feedback: Neither correlation coefficient is a perfect +1.0, so something besides shared DNA must also affect IQ scores. This study only includes people raised apart, so it can’t rule out the influence of the families they grew up in.
  • D. correlational research is not useful in looking at a variable such as intelligence.
    Feedback: This is untrue. We can use correlational research for pretty much anything! (And it would probably be unethical to carry out an experiment that made people more or less intelligent.)

Researchers in Ontario conducted a twin study exploring the genetic basis of attitudes by comparing identical and fraternal twins using data from 672 adults in two twin studies. Participants completed a survey assessing how favorably they felt about various topics, including controversial social issues, personal activities, and lifestyle preferences. Results showed stronger correlations in attitudes among identical twins (r = 0.20 to r = 0.55, median r = 0.42) than fraternal twins (r = -0.01 to r = 0.38, median r = 0.23), indicating a genetic influence.

Identical twins showed the highest similarity in attitudes toward intellectual and physical activities, like reading books (r = 0.55), playing organized sports (r = 0.52), roller coaster rides (r = 0.50), and loud music (r = 0.53). In comparison, the similarity between fraternal twins on these topics was much lower (e.g., reading books: r = 0.24, organized sports: r = 0.10, roller coaster rides: r = 0.31, loud music: r = 0.49). Attitudes like “getting along well with others” (r = 0.20 for identical twins; r = 0.19 for fraternal twins) were less influenced by genetics. The findings suggest that while genetics play a significant role in shaping attitudes, environmental factors also contribute to individual differences.

10. Which of the following research findings best supports the claim that there is a genetic basis for attitudes?

Measures: Objective 3 · Buzz import number 12

  • A. Identical twins’ attitudes toward loud music are more strongly correlated (r = 0.53) than their attitudes toward roller coaster rides (r = 0.50).
    Feedback: To make a claim about the genetic basis for attitudes, we need to compare identical twins to fraternal twins.
  • B. Both identical twins’ (r = 0.53) and fraternal twins’ (r = 0.49) attitudes about loud music are more strongly correlated than both groups’ attitudes about getting along well with others (r = 0.20 for identical twins; r = 0.19 for fraternal twins).
    Feedback: To claim that there is a genetic basis for attitudes, the correlation coefficient for a specific topic should be stronger for identical twins than it is for fraternal twins.
  • C. Attitudes of identical twins about getting along well with others have a higher association (r = 0.20) than attitudes of fraternal twins about getting along with others (r = 0.19).
    Feedback: There is not enough of a difference (0.01) between the correlation coefficients to reasonably claim that there is a genetic basis for attitudes. We can consider these correlation coefficients to be about the same.
  • D. Attitudes of identical twins toward roller coaster rides have a higher association (r = 0.50) than attitudes of fraternal twins toward roller coaster rides (r = 0.31). ✔ (correct)
    Feedback: Correct! This finding would support the claim that there is a genetic basis for attitudes.

    Feedback: A family history doesn’t make a disorder certain. Think about what it means to inherit a tendency rather than a guaranteed outcome.
  • B. Nadia’s family history tells us nothing about her risk, because only environment influences alcohol use.

    Feedback: Correct! A genetic predisposition raises the chance of developing a trait or condition, but it doesn’t guarantee it. Environmental factors, like stress, support, and access to alcohol, also play a role.

    Feedback: This reflects a misunderstanding of how inheritance works. Think about what having relatives with a condition suggests about a person’s risk.

13. (New question) Many people feel a jolt of fear when something suddenly moves in the grass near their feet, even in places where there are no dangerous animals. Which explanation would an evolutionary psychologist most likely give for this reaction?

Measures: Objective 2 · Buzz import number 15

  • B. The fear comes from a frightening childhood memory that has been pushed out of conscious awareness.

14. (New question) Which statement about natural selection is accurate?

Measures: Objective 2 · Buzz import number 16

  • A. Traits that helped individuals survive and reproduce tend to become more common in a population over many generations. ✔ (correct)
    Feedback: Correct! Individuals with helpful traits tend to leave more offspring, so the genes behind those traits are more likely to be passed on and, over generations, become more common.
  • B. Individuals change their own genes during their lifetimes to adapt to their environments.
    Feedback: Natural selection doesn’t change an individual’s genes during their lifetime. Think about what changes over many generations.
  • C. Every human behavior exists because it once helped our ancestors survive.
    Feedback: This statement is overstated. Evolutionary explanations are hypotheses, and not every behavior must have an evolutionary purpose.
  • D. Natural selection explains why people differ from one another, but not why people share similar tendencies.
    Feedback: Evolutionary psychologists focus mainly on the tendencies that humans share. Think about what natural selection does to a population over time.

16. (New question) Researchers find that identical twins raised in different homes have very similar levels of confidence. A critic argues that these results may overstate the role of genes. Which point best supports the critic’s argument?

Measures: Objective 3 · Buzz import number 18

  • A. Identical twins don’t actually share the same genes.
    Feedback: Identical twins develop from a single fertilized egg, so they do share all of their genes. Think about what else twins raised apart might have in common.
  • B. A study of twins is an experiment, so its results can’t be generalized.
    Feedback: Twin studies aren’t experiments, because researchers can’t randomly assign people to genes or families. Think about what else twins raised apart might have in common.
  • C. Because identical twins look alike, other people may treat them in similar ways, which could create similar environments even in different homes. ✔ (correct)
    Feedback: Correct! Similar treatment from others is part of each twin’s environment, so some of the twins’ similarity could come from experience rather than genes.
  • D. Twin studies only include two people at a time, so they can’t produce a correlation.
    Feedback: Twin studies include many pairs of twins, and researchers commonly report correlations between twins. Think about what else twins raised apart might have in common.
